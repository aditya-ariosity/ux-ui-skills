# Visual Direction

Visual design should make priority, relationship, state, action, and brand character perceptible. Correctness alone does not create a memorable interface.

## Direction contract

Define these before high-fidelity creation:

- **Purpose:** what the interface must help users perceive or accomplish.
- **Tone:** a precise character, not a stack of adjectives.
- **Composition:** dominant structure, focal area, rhythm, and asymmetry or restraint.
- **Density:** how much information is visible and why that matches frequency and expertise.
- **Type:** roles, contrast, reading behavior, numeric needs, and language coverage.
- **Color:** semantic roles, brand behavior, surface hierarchy, and contrast requirements.
- **Imagery and icons:** content purpose, style, optical consistency, and fallback behavior.
- **Motion:** what change, causality, or orientation motion communicates.
- **Signature:** one recognisable element or interaction that belongs to this product.
- **Avoid:** clichés, conflicting styles, and treatments that weaken the task or brand.

## Generate alternatives deliberately

When exploration is useful, vary the underlying strategy rather than recoloring one layout. A direction may prioritize:

- operational clarity and controlled density;
- editorial authority and narrative pacing;
- spatial exploration and relationship discovery;
- direct manipulation and tool-like efficiency;
- calm review and high-consequence confirmation.

For each direction, explain task fit, brand fit, accessibility risk, implementation cost, and likely failure mode. Do not generate alternatives after a direction has been approved unless new evidence invalidates it.

## Anti-slop checks

Reject unexplained defaults such as:

- generic purple-to-pink AI gradients;
- a hero plus repeated feature-card grid for every artifact;
- glass surfaces without a depth or context function;
- excessive rounded cards that fragment related information;
- large display type that consumes task space;
- arbitrary dark mode based on industry;
- stock illustrations that add no product meaning;
- animation attached to every visible element;
- metrics, testimonials, or trust marks that are not real.

Do not pursue novelty by reducing clarity. Distinctiveness should come from a coherent product-specific idea.
